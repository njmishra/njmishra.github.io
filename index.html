What a lattice is

Take a few vectors, add up whole-number multiples of them every way you can, and the points you land on are a lattice.

A lattice has no preferred basis. The same set of points can be described by a short, nearly perpendicular basis or by a long, skewed one, and converting between them is where the difficulty hides. With a good basis the natural questions are easy. With a bad one, in high dimension, nobody knows how to answer them in reasonable time — and, unlike factoring, that remains true when the attacker owns a quantum computer. Cryptography built on lattices is a bet on exactly that gap.

The shortest vector problem
Given a bad basis, find the shortest nonzero point of the lattice — the amber vector in the diagram. Reducing a basis and solving this problem are the same task seen from two sides.
Learning With Errors
Hide a secret in a pile of linear equations, then nudge every answer by a small error. Recovering the secret turns out to be a lattice problem in disguise: find the lattice point nearest a target you can see.
Module-LWE
The same idea over a polynomial ring, which shrinks keys from megabytes to kilobytes and makes the arithmetic fast. ML-KEM and ML-DSA, the schemes NIST standardised in 2024, both rest on it. The open worry is whether that extra ring structure also gives something back to the attacker.

Research

I work in post-quantum cryptography, on the lattice problems that the next generation of encryption and signature standards is built on. Most of my research is cryptanalysis of Module-LWE, the structured variant that keeps keys small enough to be practical: the extra algebraic structure is what makes these schemes fast, and my question is whether an attacker can turn that same structure into an attack cheaper than the general lattice case allows. One strand looks at the Galois symmetry of module lattices and finds that it gives the primal attack no advantage at all — the structure is free for the designer and useless to the adversary. A second strand is concrete security: not whether a problem is hard in the asymptotic limit, but what the best known attacks actually cost in bits at the parameters a real scheme ships with, including schemes for homomorphic encryption. A third applies the same key-protection questions to biometrics, binding a cryptographic key to several biometric traits at once without burying it among decoy points.
